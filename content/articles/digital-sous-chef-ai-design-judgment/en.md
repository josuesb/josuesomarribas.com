<p dir="auto">AI is useful in design when it handles repetitive production work without replacing the strategic decisions a designer is paid to make.</p><p dir="auto">Think of it as a digital sous-chef. It can chop, sort, prep, clean, summarize, and suggest. It should not decide the menu.</p><h2 dir="auto">Where AI is genuinely useful</h2><p dir="auto">The best use cases are usually boring. That is a good sign.</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Drafting alt text for images.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Summarizing workshop notes.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Cleaning interview transcripts.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Grouping feedback into themes.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Generating first-pass content variations.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Creating checklists from messy project notes.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Turning a rough outline into a structured draft.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">These tasks matter, but they are not where the deepest design judgment lives. They are the carpentry around the decision.</p><h2 dir="auto">Where AI gets dangerous</h2><p dir="auto">AI becomes risky when teams use it to skip interpretation.</p><p dir="auto">A summary of research notes is not an insight. A list of interface ideas is not a strategy. A generated alt text draft is not automatically accessible. A polished paragraph is not automatically true.</p><p dir="auto">The tool can compress material. The designer still has to decide what matters.</p><h2 dir="auto">A practical AI workflow for designers</h2><p dir="auto">Use AI in three passes.</p><h3 dir="auto">1. Production pass</h3><p dir="auto">Ask AI to do the repetitive work: clean notes, extract quotes, generate field descriptions, draft alt text, or format a report.</p><h3 dir="auto">2. Judgment pass</h3><p dir="auto">Review the output yourself. Remove hallucinated claims, weak language, generic patterns, and anything that sounds confident without evidence.</p><h3 dir="auto">3. Design pass</h3><p dir="auto">Turn the cleaned material into a decision: what changes, what stays, what needs testing, and what should be ignored.</p><h2 dir="auto">Alt text is a good example</h2><p dir="auto">AI can draft alt text quickly, but the right alt text depends on context. The same image can need different descriptions depending on whether it is decorative, instructional, emotional, or functional.</p><p dir="auto">A photo of a dashboard might need no alt text in one context, a short description in another, or a detailed explanation if the image carries information the article depends on.</p><p dir="auto">The AI can suggest the sentence. The designer must decide the purpose.</p><h2 dir="auto">The rule I use</h2><p dir="auto">Delegate the work that has a pattern. Keep the work that has consequences.</p><p dir="auto">Generating ten title options is fine. Choosing the positioning is yours. Summarizing a workshop is fine. Deciding what problem the team should solve next is yours.</p><h2 dir="auto">The quiet benefit</h2><p dir="auto">Used well, AI gives designers more time for the uncomfortable part of the job: making decisions with incomplete information.</p><p dir="auto">That is where the value is. Not in producing more interface noise, but in protecting the thinking from being buried under admin work.</p>
