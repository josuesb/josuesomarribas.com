<p>A team proudly reports that 72% of its experiments win. That number may describe exceptional idea quality. More often, it describes a system that chooses safe hypotheses, stops at convenient moments, promotes secondary metrics, or quietly loses inconclusive results.</p>

<p>A high win rate is not the same as a strong experimentation culture. Experiments exist to change decisions under uncertainty. If the program rewards only positive movement, people will adapt their behavior until the dashboard produces it.</p>

<h2>What winner manufacturing looks like</h2>

<ul>
<li>Teams select tiny, obvious changes because ambitious hypotheses might lose.</li>
<li>Experiments stop when the graph looks favorable rather than at the planned rule.</li>
<li>A secondary metric becomes “the real KPI” after the primary stays flat.</li>
<li>Null and negative results disappear from reviews and documentation.</li>
<li>The number of launched tests matters more than their validity or decisions.</li>
<li>Every local lift ships without replication, durability checks, or guardrails.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these behaviors requires dishonesty. Incentives do the work. Tell people their performance depends on wins, and intelligent people will redefine winning.</p>

<h2>Measure decisions, not green dashboards</h2>

<p>The useful output of an experimentation program is a better decision: ship, stop, simplify, investigate, replicate, or change direction. A valid flat result that prevents an expensive rollout can be more valuable than a positive click lift that changes nothing important.</p>

<p>A healthier scorecard includes:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothesis quality:</strong> proportion grounded in observable evidence and a plausible mechanism.</li>
<li><strong>Design validity:</strong> proportion completed without integrity, assignment, instrumentation, or stopping-rule failures.</li>
<li><strong>Decision yield:</strong> experiments that materially changed a product decision, including decisions not to ship.</li>
<li><strong>Learning velocity:</strong> time from an important uncertainty to documented evidence and a decision.</li>
<li><strong>Replication:</strong> strategically important effects that survive a repeated test or post-launch validation.</li>
<li><strong>Documentation quality:</strong> results that remain findable, interpretable, and reusable by another team.</li>
</ul>

<h2>A four-level maturity model</h2>

<h3>Level 1: Activity</h3>
<p>The organization counts tests. Success means launching more. Hypotheses are inconsistent, metrics change during analysis, and knowledge lives in presentations or people’s memory.</p>

<h3>Level 2: Reliability</h3>
<p>Pre-launch briefs, QA, sample planning, guardrails, and stopping rules become normal. The team can trust more results, but the roadmap may still treat experiments as validation services.</p>

<h3>Level 3: Learning</h3>
<p>Null and negative outcomes are visible. Research feeds hypotheses. Results enter a searchable repository. Reviews focus on decisions and mechanisms, not only winners.</p>

<h3>Level 4: Adaptation</h3>
<p>Evidence changes strategy, not just components. Important results are replicated, long-term effects are checked, and leaders revise their own proposals when data disagree.</p>

<h2>A fictional example</h2>

<p>Imagine a product group expected to deliver four winning tests each quarter. It chooses predictable copy and layout changes, launches many variants, and reports the most favorable movement. The win-rate target is met, yet no customer problem becomes meaningfully easier.</p>

<p>The group replaces that goal with three commitments: every test must trace back to evidence, every completed test must produce a documented decision, and every high-impact win must receive a durability check. Test volume falls. The percentage of positive results also falls. But duplicate work drops, larger ideas receive better preparation, and leadership can see which assumptions changed. The program looks worse on the old scorecard and better in reality.</p>

<h2>Questions for the monthly retrospective</h2>

<ul>
<li>Which decision changed because of evidence this month?</li>
<li>Which result surprised us, and what mechanism might explain it?</li>
<li>Which experiment was invalid or underpowered, and why did the process allow it?</li>
<li>What did we learn from null and negative outcomes?</li>
<li>Which shipped effect still needs replication or long-term monitoring?</li>
<li>Which hypothesis should research clarify before we spend more traffic?</li>
<li>Did any incentive encourage selective interpretation?</li>
</ul>

<h2>Actions for leaders</h2>

<ol>
<li>Remove win rate from individual performance goals.</li>
<li>Require predeclared metrics, thresholds, guardrails, and stopping rules.</li>
<li>Review invalid tests with the same seriousness as failed releases.</li>
<li>Make null and negative results easy to discover.</li>
<li>Celebrate decisions changed by evidence, including killed ideas.</li>
<li>Reserve replication for surprising, costly, or strategic effects.</li>
</ol>

<p>A mature experimentation culture does not make losing comfortable by lowering standards. It makes honest learning valuable by raising them. The goal is not to prove that the team has good ideas. It is to build a system that discovers which ideas deserve reality.</p>
