<p dir="auto">Every analytics review I've sat through has the same moment. Someone opens GA4, clicks into Acquisition, and finds a wall of <strong>direct / (none)</strong> that explains nothing. The newsletter went out. The LinkedIn post got real engagement. Somebody shared the article in a Slack community. The traffic clearly happened — and the report collapsed all of it into "direct," which is analytics shorthand for "no idea."</p><p dir="auto">UTM parameters exist to fix exactly that. They're the cheapest, oldest, most reliable attribution tool on the web, and they're also one of the easiest things to get quietly wrong. A single uppercase letter splits your campaign data in two. A tag on an internal link rewrites a visitor's origin story. A redirect strips your parameters before GA4 ever sees them.</p><p dir="auto">This guide covers the three things I wish every marketer and designer had on one page: what UTM parameters actually are, how they interact with SEO (including the ways they can hurt it), and a step-by-step process to verify your tags in GA4 before you ship a campaign. The GA4 walkthrough uses my own site, josuesomarribas.com, so you can see the real flow instead of an idealized one.</p><h2 dir="auto">What are UTM parameters?</h2><p dir="auto">UTM parameters are five standardized tags you append to a URL's query string so analytics tools can attribute a visit to a specific source, channel, and campaign. They don't change what the page displays. They change what your analytics platform knows about how the visitor arrived.</p><p dir="auto">The name is a fossil: UTM stands for <em>Urchin Tracking Module</em>. Urchin was the analytics company <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urchin_(software)">Google acquired in 2005</a> and turned into Google Analytics. The product is long gone, but the parameter names have survived three generations of Google Analytics, including GA4.</p><p dir="auto">Here's a fully tagged URL using my own site as the example:</p><p dir="auto"><em>https://josuesomarribas.com/<strong>?utm_source=linkedin&amp;utm_medium=social&amp;utm_campaign=utm-guide-launch&amp;utm_content=personal-profile</strong></em></p><p dir="auto">Everything after the question mark is invisible to the visitor's experience and very visible to GA4. The five parameters each answer one question:</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>utm_source</strong> — where does the traffic come from? A specific platform or property: <em>linkedin</em>, <em>google</em>, <em>newsletter</em>, <em>slack</em>. Treat it as mandatory.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>utm_medium</strong> — how does it arrive? The channel type: <em>social</em>, <em>email</em>, <em>cpc</em>, <em>referral</em>. Also treat it as mandatory, because GA4's channel classification depends on it.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>utm_campaign</strong> — which initiative is this? The project or push: <em>utm-guide-launch</em>, <em>q3-promo</em>, <em>blog-distribution</em>.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>utm_content</strong> — which specific link or creative? Useful when one campaign has several links: <em>hero-button</em>, <em>footer-link</em>, <em>ad-variant-b</em>. Optional, and the backbone of creative-level A/B comparisons.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>utm_term</strong> — which paid keyword triggered this? Mostly a paid-search field, and largely handled automatically if you use Google Ads auto-tagging. Optional.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">Source and medium are technically optional in the sense that the URL still works without them. Practically, they're a pair: GA4 uses the combination to classify traffic into channels, and a tagged URL with a missing or invented medium tends to land in <strong>Unassigned</strong> — the channel report's junk drawer.</p><h2 dir="auto">How GA4 reads a tagged URL</h2><p dir="auto">When someone lands on a tagged URL, GA4 captures the parameters with the first <em>page_view</em> and stores them in a few different dimensions. Three matter for everyday work:</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Session source / medium</strong> — the attribution for that session. This is what you'll check in most reports.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Session campaign</strong> — the campaign name attached to that session.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>First user source / medium</strong> — set once, on a person's first-ever visit, and never overwritten. This is how GA4 remembers which channel originally acquired a user.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">On top of these, GA4 maps each source/medium pair to a channel using <a href="https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9756891">a fixed set of rules</a>. <em>linkedin / social</em> becomes Organic Social. <em>newsletter / email</em> becomes Email. A creative-but-nonstandard medium like <em>linkedin-post</em> matches no rule and falls into Unassigned, which is how perfectly good campaigns disappear from channel reports.</p><p dir="auto">One detail worth knowing if you came from Universal Analytics: in GA4, clicking a new tagged link mid-session does not start a new session. The campaign information is processed differently, which is one more reason the testing workflow later in this post checks specific dimensions instead of assuming the old behavior.</p><h2 dir="auto">Do UTM parameters affect SEO?</h2><p dir="auto">The direct answer: UTM parameters are not a ranking signal. Google doesn't rank a tagged URL higher or lower because of its tags. The real relationship between UTMs and SEO is structural — a URL with parameters is, technically, a different URL, and that has consequences you need to manage.</p><h3 dir="auto">The duplicate-URL problem (and the canonical fix)</h3><p dir="auto">Every tagged variant of a page is a separate crawlable URL. If tagged links to your site accumulate across newsletters, social posts, and partner sites, Google can discover and occasionally index those variants, splitting signals between <em>/blog/my-post</em> and <em>/blog/my-post?utm_source=newsletter</em>.</p><p dir="auto">The fix is boring and reliable: a <strong>self-referencing canonical tag</strong> on every page, pointing to the clean URL. Google's own documentation on <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls">consolidating duplicate URLs</a> lists this as the primary tool for exactly this case. With the canonical in place, every tagged variant consolidates back to the clean URL, and the duplicate problem stays theoretical.</p><p dir="auto">If your site runs on Framer, like mine, self-referencing canonicals ship by default — but verify it anyway: open any blog post, view the page source, and search for <em>rel="canonical"</em>. The href should be the clean URL without parameters. Two more hygiene rules: never put tagged URLs in your sitemap, and never use a tagged URL when you link to your own site from places you control.</p><h3 dir="auto">Never put UTM parameters on internal links</h3><p dir="auto">This is the single most damaging UTM mistake, and it's common enough to deserve its own section. If you tag a banner on your homepage with <em>utm_source=homepage</em>, every visitor who clicks it gets their real attribution overwritten. The person who found you through organic search now reports as <em>homepage / banner</em>. Your acquisition data stops describing acquisition.</p><p dir="auto">It also creates crawlable parameter URLs across your entire site, which is the duplicate-URL problem above, self-inflicted at scale. If you want to measure clicks on internal elements, that's what GA4 events are for — UTM parameters are strictly for traffic arriving from <em>outside</em> your domain.</p><h3 dir="auto">Where UTMs genuinely help your SEO work</h3><p dir="auto">Used correctly, UTMs don't boost rankings — they protect the data you use to make SEO decisions:</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>They keep your organic report honest.</strong> Untagged distribution traffic gets misattributed as direct or referral, and some of it bleeds into numbers you compare organic against. When everything you control is tagged, what's left in Organic Search is actually organic, and trends in it mean something.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>They show which distribution makes content travel.</strong> A post that takes off after a newsletter send behaves differently than one that grows on search alone. Knowing which channel lit the fire tells you where to push the next piece — and content that travels is content that earns links, which is the part Google does reward.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>They connect content to conversions.</strong> Pair campaign dimensions with conversion events and you can see which articles bring readers who actually do something. That's the difference between optimizing for traffic and optimizing for outcomes.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>They cover the discovery that search reports can't see.</strong> A growing share of discovery happens in AI assistants, newsletters, and private communities — places Search Console will never show you. Tagging every link you control (profiles, signatures, newsletters) is the cheapest way to make that invisible distribution measurable. I've written more about that shift in <a href="https://josuesomarribas.com/blog/geo-vs-seo-ai-search">GEO vs SEO</a> and <a href="https://josuesomarribas.com/blog/geo-vs-seo-ai-search">how to track AI visibility</a>.</p></li></ul><h2 dir="auto">Naming conventions that keep your data clean</h2><p dir="auto">GA4 is case-sensitive and literal. <em>Summer-2026</em> and <em>summer-2026</em> are two different campaigns in your reports, forever. Most UTM damage isn't caused by missing tags — it's caused by inconsistent ones. Seven rules cover almost everything:</p><ol dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Lowercase everything, always.</strong> It's the cheapest insurance against split data.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Hyphens, not spaces.</strong> A space becomes <em>%20</em> in the URL and ugly noise in your reports. <em>summer-launch</em>, never <em>Summer Launch</em>.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>One canonical name per source.</strong> Pick <em>linkedin</em> and never type <em>LinkedIn</em>, <em>linked-in</em>, or <em>linkedin.com</em> again.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>A fixed vocabulary for medium.</strong> Stick to values GA4's channel rules recognize: <em>social</em>, <em>email</em>, <em>cpc</em>, <em>referral</em>. Inventing mediums is how traffic ends up Unassigned.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Campaign names that say initiative and timeframe.</strong> <em>utm-guide-launch</em> or <em>newsletter-2026-06</em> beat <em>campaign1</em> every time someone reads the report six months later.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Document the convention.</strong> A shared sheet with allowed sources, mediums, and the campaign naming pattern. The convention only works if it survives contact with your teammates.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Validate before you ship.</strong> Check the encoding, check for duplicates, check the case. I built a <a href="https://utm.josuesomarribas.com/" target="_blank">free UTM Parameter Builder &amp; Validator</a> that does exactly this — it flags spaces, uppercase, existing parameters, and broken URLs before they reach your reports. Google's own <a href="https://ga-dev-tools.google/campaign-url-builder/">Campaign URL Builder</a> handles the assembly too, though it won't warn you about naming problems.</p></li></ol><h2 dir="auto">How to test UTM parameters in GA4 (step by step)</h2><p dir="auto">Building the URL is half the job. The other half is verifying that GA4 actually receives and classifies it the way you expect — before the campaign goes out, not after. This is the exact process I use on josuesomarribas.com; it works identically on any site with a GA4 property.</p><h3 dir="auto">Step 1: Build a tagged test URL</h3><p dir="auto">Take a real page and tag it with a campaign name you'll recognize as a test. Adding a <em>-test</em> suffix makes it easy to find now and filter out later:</p><img alt="" src="/images/framer-migrated/5f3O9hCnr2t98mmtgkbQgjhumIY.webp"><p dir="auto"><em>Result: https://josuesomarribas.com/<strong>?utm_source=linkedin&amp;utm_medium=social&amp;utm_campaign=utm-guide-test</strong></em></p><p dir="auto">Use a medium from GA4's standard vocabulary (<em>social</em> here) so you can also verify channel classification in step 6.</p><h3 dir="auto">Step 2: Visit the URL in a private window</h3><img alt="" src="/images/framer-migrated/j28mlmJzRyd041mpjjMn2pfQEJ0.webp"><p dir="auto">Open an incognito/private window and paste the full tagged URL. The private window avoids any signed-in state that might muddy the data. One caveat: if your GA4 property has an internal traffic filter that excludes your IP, your visit may never appear — switch your phone to cellular data and open the link there instead.</p><h3 dir="auto">Step 3: Watch it arrive in Realtime</h3><p dir="auto">In GA4, go to <strong>Reports → Realtime</strong>. Your visit should appear within a minute. Find the traffic source card and switch its dimension to <strong>Session source / medium</strong> — you're looking for <em>linkedin / social</em>. Flip the same card to <strong>Session campaign</strong> and confirm the exact campaign name, character for character.</p><p dir="auto"><em>📸 [SCREENSHOT — GA4 Realtime report on the josuesomarribas.com property, traffic source card showing session source/medium = linkedin / social]</em></p><p dir="auto"><em>📸 [SCREENSHOT — Same Realtime card switched to Session campaign, showing utm-guide-test]</em></p><h3 dir="auto">Step 4 (optional): Inspect the raw event in DebugView</h3><p dir="auto">If Realtime shows something unexpected, <a href="https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7201382">DebugView</a> shows you the raw events as they arrive. Enable debug mode (the Google Analytics Debugger browser extension is the fastest route), open the tagged URL again, then go to <strong>Admin → DebugView</strong> and click the incoming <em>page_view</em> event. The campaign parameters are listed right on the event — if they're wrong here, the problem is in your URL, not in your reports.</p><p dir="auto"><em>📸 [SCREENSHOT — DebugView showing the page_view event with the campaign parameters expanded]</em></p><h3 dir="auto">Step 5: Confirm it in Traffic acquisition (24–48 hours later)</h3><p dir="auto">Realtime proves the tag arrived; the standard reports prove it was processed. GA4's <a href="https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11198161">data freshness</a> means standard reports can lag, so check back within a day or two. Go to <strong>Reports → Acquisition → Traffic acquisition</strong>, set the primary dimension to <strong>Session source / medium</strong>, and search for <em>linkedin</em>. Add <strong>Session campaign</strong> as a secondary dimension to see your test campaign on its own row.</p><p dir="auto"><em>📸 [SCREENSHOT — Traffic acquisition report filtered to linkedin / social, with Session campaign as secondary dimension showing utm-guide-test]</em></p><h3 dir="auto">Step 6: Check the channel classification</h3><p dir="auto">Still in Traffic acquisition, switch the primary dimension to <strong>Session default channel group</strong>. Your test session should sit under <strong>Organic Social</strong> — that's the rule <em>linkedin / social</em> maps to. If it landed in Unassigned instead, your medium isn't in GA4's recognized vocabulary, and that's worth fixing before any real campaign uses it.</p><p dir="auto"><em>📸 [SCREENSHOT — Traffic acquisition by default channel group, with the test session under Organic Social]</em></p><p dir="auto">A passing test looks like this:</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Realtime shows the exact source/medium pair you tagged.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>The campaign name matches character for character — no case variants, no encoding artifacts.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>The session classifies into the channel you intended, not Unassigned.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>The clean URL (no parameters) is what appears as the landing page, with your canonical tag pointing to it.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">When you're done, the <em>-test</em> suffix earns its keep: exclude those campaigns from any exploration or report you share, and your experiment never pollutes real numbers.</p><h2 dir="auto">Common UTM mistakes that corrupt GA4 data</h2><p dir="auto">Almost every broken acquisition report I've seen traces back to one of these:</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Case variants.</strong> <em>linkedin</em> and <em>LinkedIn</em> are different rows in every GA4 report. Lowercase is non-negotiable.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Spaces and special characters.</strong> They encode into <em>%20</em> and friends, and your clean campaign name becomes report garbage.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Missing or invented mediums.</strong> No medium, or a creative one like <em>social-post</em>, sends sessions to Unassigned where nobody looks.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>UTMs on internal links.</strong> Overwrites real attribution and floods your own site with parameter URLs. Use events instead.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>Redirects that strip parameters.</strong> Link shorteners, ad platforms, and redirect chains sometimes drop the query string. Always test the final URL — the one users actually land on — not the one you typed.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p><strong>One campaign name reused forever.</strong> If everything is <em>utm_campaign=newsletter</em>, you have a channel label, not a campaign. Add the timeframe or the initiative.</p></li></ul><h2 dir="auto">FAQ</h2><h3 dir="auto">Do UTM parameters affect Google rankings?</h3><p dir="auto">No — UTM parameters are not a ranking factor. The only SEO risk is structural: tagged URLs are technically separate URLs, and without a self-referencing canonical tag they can split indexing signals. With canonicals in place (most modern platforms, including Framer, set them automatically), the risk is handled.</p><h3 dir="auto">Should I use UTM parameters on internal links?</h3><p dir="auto">No, never. Tagging internal links overwrites the visitor's real acquisition source in GA4 and creates duplicate parameter URLs across your site. If you need to measure clicks on internal elements, use GA4 events.</p><h3 dir="auto">Why does my UTM traffic show as Unassigned in GA4?</h3><p dir="auto">Usually because <em>utm_medium</em> is missing or doesn't match GA4's channel definitions. GA4 classifies channels from the source/medium pair using fixed rules — stick to standard values like <em>social</em>, <em>email</em>, <em>cpc</em>, and <em>referral</em>, and the sessions will classify correctly.</p><h3 dir="auto">How long until UTM data appears in GA4?</h3><p dir="auto">Realtime shows tagged sessions within about a minute. Standard reports like Traffic acquisition depend on GA4's data freshness and typically take 24–48 hours to fully process. Test in Realtime; confirm in standard reports the next day.</p><h3 dir="auto">What's the difference between utm_source and utm_medium?</h3><p dir="auto">Source is <em>who</em> sent the traffic — the specific platform or property, like <em>linkedin</em> or <em>newsletter</em>. Medium is <em>how</em> it arrived — the channel type, like <em>social</em> or <em>email</em>. GA4 needs both to classify the session into a channel.</p><h3 dir="auto">Does Google index URLs with UTM parameters?</h3><p dir="auto">It can, if tagged URLs are linked publicly and no canonical tag exists. A self-referencing canonical on every page consolidates all tagged variants back to the clean URL, which is Google's recommended fix for duplicate URL variants.</p><h2 dir="auto">Boring tracking is good tracking</h2><p dir="auto">UTM parameters aren't sophisticated, and that's the point.
