<h1 dir="auto">How to Run User Research When You Have No Budget and No Recruiter</h1><p dir="auto">User research does not become fake just because you do not have a lab, a recruiter, a gift-card budget, or a room with one-way glass that makes everyone feel like they are in a police drama. It becomes fake when the questions are weak, the sample is lazy, and the team treats five opinions as a universal law.</p><p dir="auto">The Google Trends signal you shared is worth taking seriously: “user research” moved from 17 to 60 in the last 12 months comparison, and the site already has visibility around traveler research queries. Trends is not search volume. Google explains that Trends data is normalized on a 0 to 100 scale based on the topic’s proportion of searches for the selected time and place, so it shows relative interest, not how many people searched. Still, a jump like that is a useful editorial smell. Something is moving.</p><p dir="auto">This article is for the version of UX research most people actually need: small, practical, slightly messy, but useful enough to change a decision. It connects naturally with <a href="/blog/user-research-building-products-people-actually-need">User Research: Building Products People Actually Need</a>, <a href="/blog/how-to-do-user-research-for-travelers-the-complete-guide">How to Do User Research for Travelers</a>, and <a href="/blog/how-user-research-shaped-my-travel-platform">How User Research Shaped My Travel Platform</a>.</p><h2 dir="auto">Start with the decision, not the method</h2><p dir="auto">Bad research starts with a method looking for a reason to exist. “Let’s run interviews” sounds responsible, but it is vague. Interviews for what? To choose a feature? To understand language? To find objections? To map a journey? A research plan should begin with the decision that will change if the findings are strong enough.</p><p dir="auto">A useful framing is simple: “We need to decide X. We believe Y. We are unsure about Z. Research should reduce that uncertainty.” That is less glamorous than a design thinking mural, but it keeps the work honest.</p><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>If you need to understand motivations, run interviews.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>If you need to see whether people can complete a task, run usability tests.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>If you need to compare demand across options, use surveys carefully.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>If you need to see real behavior, look at analytics, support tickets, search queries, and session recordings.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">This matters because users are good at describing problems, constraints, language, and context. They are much worse at predicting future behavior. Ask “Would you use this?” and you will often get a polite lie. Not malicious. Just human. Ask “Tell me about the last time this happened” and the conversation gets better immediately.</p><h2 dir="auto">Use the sources you already have</h2><p dir="auto">A no-budget research workflow should not start by hunting strangers on the internet. Start with existing signals. Support conversations, contact form messages, sales calls, cancellation reasons, failed searches, Hotjar recordings, analytics drop-offs, review comments, and email replies can all reveal what users are trying to do and where the product is making them work too hard.</p><p dir="auto">The U.S. HHS Research-Based Web Design &amp; Usability Guidelines make a similar point in a more formal way: teams should use all available resources to understand user requirements, including support lines, surveys, interviews, user groups, and other exchanges with users. The guideline also warns against relying too heavily on intermediaries. Translation: do not let the loudest stakeholder cosplay as the user.</p><p dir="auto">For a small site, this can be brutally simple. Open the last 50 contact messages. Tag each one by intent. Open analytics and find the pages with high impressions and low clicks. Read the queries. Watch 10 recordings where users abandon the form. Now you have a research backlog without spending a cent. Fancy? No. Useful? Usually.</p><h2 dir="auto">Recruit from friction points</h2><p dir="auto">When there is no recruiter, recruit where the friction already happens. People who just contacted support, abandoned a form, left a review, clicked an internal search result, or replied to a newsletter are more relevant than random LinkedIn volunteers. The sample is biased, obviously. But all samples are biased. The adult thing is to name the bias and use the data for the right decision.</p><p dir="auto">For example, if you are researching a travel planning tool, backpacker forum users may be useful for language and edge cases, but they are not representative of families booking one annual trip. If you are improving an Airbnb check-in message, recent guests are better than generic “travelers,” because they have lived the moment you are studying. That distinction saves you from research theatre, which is research with nicer sticky notes.</p><h2 dir="auto">A simple five-person interview workflow</h2><p dir="auto">You can run useful interviews with five participants if the scope is narrow. The classic mistake is asking five people about the whole product. That gives you mush. Ask five people about one painful decision, one workflow, or one moment where expectations break.</p><h3 dir="auto">1. Write the research question</h3><p dir="auto">Example: “What information do first-time visitors need before they trust this booking page?” That is specific enough to guide questions and broad enough to reveal surprises.</p><h3 dir="auto">2. Define who counts as a participant</h3><p dir="auto">Do not write “users.” Write the actual criteria. First-time visitors who considered booking but did not complete. Past guests who checked in without staff help. Designers who recently moved a project from Figma to Framer. The narrower the criteria, the cleaner the findings.</p><h3 dir="auto">3. Ask about the past, not the fantasy future</h3><p dir="auto">Good questions sound almost boring. “Tell me about the last time you tried to book a place like this.” “What did you compare?” “What made you hesitate?” “What did you look for and not find?” These questions pull facts and stories out of people. They do not ask them to become product managers for 30 minutes.</p><h3 dir="auto">4. Tag evidence, not vibes</h3><p dir="auto">After each session, write down direct quotes, observed behaviors, repeated objections, and unanswered questions. Keep the raw notes visible. When a stakeholder later says “users want this,” you can ask which user, in which moment, saying what. A tiny dose of accountability. Very medicinal.</p><h3 dir="auto">5. Turn findings into decisions</h3><p dir="auto">A research report that does not change a decision is just documentation wearing a nice shirt. End with recommended actions, confidence level, and open risks. Say what you would change now, what needs testing, and what should not be touched yet.</p><h2 dir="auto">When to use usability testing instead</h2><p dir="auto">Interviews explain why people think, hesitate, and choose. Usability testing shows whether the interface lets them do the thing. They are not interchangeable. If someone says “I understand the flow” during an interview, lovely. Put the prototype in front of them and watch. Interfaces have a special talent for humiliating assumptions.</p><p dir="auto">For no-budget usability testing, use a prototype, a staging page, or even a clickable Figma flow. Give participants a realistic task. Do not lead them. Do not explain the UI. If they get stuck, note where and why. HHS usability guidance emphasizes iterative design and testing before and after changes, which is a boring recommendation because it is correct.</p><h2 dir="auto">What to do with traveler-specific queries</h2><p dir="auto">The existing ranking for traveler research is a small opening. Do not waste it by writing another generic “how to do UX research” post. A satellite post should speak directly to the messy travel context: limited time, changing locations, poor Wi-Fi, language differences, device constraints, trust issues, and situational stress.</p><p dir="auto">The angle could be: “How to Do User Research for Travelers When Everyone Is Moving.” That post can link back to the broader research article and forward to travel UX content like <a href="/blog/designing-for-attention-degradation-ux-for-bags-sun-glare-and-3g">Designing for Attention Degradation</a>. This is where SEO and product thinking finally sit at the same table without throwing forks.</p><h2 dir="auto">The no-budget research checklist</h2><ul dir="auto"><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Name the decision before choosing the method.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Use existing evidence before recruiting anyone.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Recruit from the moment where friction happens.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Ask about recent behavior, not imaginary future use.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Separate interview findings from usability issues.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>Document direct quotes and observed behavior.</p></li><li data-preset-tag="p"><p>End with actions, risks, and confidence level.</p></li></ul><p dir="auto">No-budget research is not second-class research. It is constrained research. That means you have to be more careful, not more dramatic. The limitation is not the lack of software. The limitation is pretending that a small messy study can answer every question. Use it to reduce risk, sharpen copy, find broken assumptions, and decide what to test next.</p><p dir="auto"><strong>Sources:</strong> <a href="https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533?hl=en">Google Trends Help, FAQ about Trends data</a>; <a href="https://newsinitiative.withgoogle.com/resources/trainings/basics-of-google-trends/">Google News Initiative, Basics of Google Trends</a>; <a href="https://www.usability.gov/sites/default/files/documents/guidelines_book.pdf">U.S. HHS Research-Based Web Design &amp; Usability Guidelines</a>; <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/">Nielsen Norman Group UX articles</a>.</p>
